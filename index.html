Research

Spacecraft data, planetary environments, solar storms

Spacecraft explore places humans simply cannot. Whether it's the composition of an atmosphere, the shape of a magnetic field, or the pattern of craters on a surface, these observatories hand us a wealth of data about the environments of Earth's planetary neighbors — and, increasingly, about the star all of them orbit.

My PhD research used data from NASA's MAVEN mission to study electron acceleration in the Martian magnetotail, the region where the Sun's magnetic field drapes around the planet much like it does around a comet. Understanding what happens there tells us how Mars interacts with the Sun, and helps us reason about other unmagnetized bodies — Venus, and exoplanets beyond our solar system.

Robotic explorers go places humans cannot, allowing us to uncover the mysteries of our solar system.

My interest in Mars research began while I was an undergraduate research assistant at the Laboratory for Atmospheric and Space Physics at CU Boulder. Now, as a postdoctoral researcher at Los Alamos National Laboratory, I've turned toward heliophysics — studying solar electron bursts and their effects, including work supporting NASA's IMAP mission.

Outreach

During the NASA SCoPE AGU/SciAct Workshop (2022), I worked with other early-career scientists and engineers to build educational materials aimed at broadening participation in the field.

As a visiting astronomer at Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park's AstroFest (2022), I gave a series of "porch talks" on life on Mars and helped guide public telescope observing across the three-day festival, reaching hundreds of visitors.

From 2013 to 2017, I worked at Fiske Planetarium — operating the 8K dome, giving talks, running the portable dome for K-12 students, and producing laser light shows — helping make the distant world of astronomy feel closer to families and visitors of all ages.

Life

Where the science comes from

I'm a Guyanese-American raised in Colorado. My path here wasn't a straight line — I started out planning a career in international affairs, then changed course when I saw how little funding existed in that field. Planetary science gave me a way to build a meaningful career without financial sacrifice, in a field where the "leaky pipeline" of academia loses too many capable students along the way.

I got here because compassionate mentors guided me as both a scientist and a person. As a first-generation higher-education student whose grandmother is illiterate, part of what drives me now is making sure students from similar backgrounds have that same guidance.

Climbing has taught me to move through unusual spaces, literally and otherwise. I wrote an essay for Rock and Ice Magazine on how climbers can actively pursue anti-racism — not an exposé of the sport, but an attempt to name a burden many climbers of color carry, and offer a few ways to lighten it.
